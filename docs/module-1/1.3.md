## The one idea

Lesson 1.2 said training nudges parameters to reduce a loss. The three kinds of learning
differ in **where the training signal comes from**:

```diagram
three-kinds-of-learning
```

| Kind | Signal | Learns to | Data you need |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Supervised** | a correct answer per example | map input → label/value | labelled examples — the expensive part |
| **Unsupervised** | none; structure in the inputs | group, compress, detect the unusual | just inputs, lots of them |
| **Reinforcement** | a reward after actions | choose actions that earn reward | an environment or a judge, and many trials |

That single column — the signal — predicts almost everything else about a technique: what
data it needs, what it can promise, and what "wrong" looks like.

## Supervised: examples with answers

Spam / not spam. Price of a house. Fraud / legitimate. Which product category. Almost every
classic ML system in production is supervised, and its quality is bounded by **the labels**:
how many, how accurate, how representative of what arrives in production. Lesson 3.2 and 3.3
build and evaluate one.

Two facts engineers underestimate:

- **Labels are the cost.** Models are cheap; getting ten thousand correctly labelled
  examples of *your* problem is the project.
- **The model learns the labels you gave it, including their mistakes and biases.** A model
  trained on last year's decisions reproduces last year's decisions.

```check
Your team wants a model to flag "urgent" support tickets. What is the first thing you need?
- A large language model
- A vector database
- * A few thousand past tickets with a reliable "urgent / not urgent" label
why: This is supervised learning; the labelled examples are the constraint. Without them there is nothing to learn from — and if the labels are inconsistent, the model learns the inconsistency.
```

## Unsupervised: structure without answers

Cluster customers by behaviour. Find the odd-one-out transaction. Compress a document into a
vector so similar documents land nearby (Lesson 2.3). No labels — the model looks for
regularities in the inputs alone. Cheap to start, harder to evaluate: "these five clusters" is
useful only if a person can name them.

## Reinforcement: act, get a reward, improve

An agent takes actions in an environment and gets a reward signal — win the game, keep the
robot upright, keep the user reading. It learns a *policy*: which action to take in which
state. Powerful, data-hungry, and hard to make safe: the policy optimises the reward you
wrote, not the intent you had. That last point resurfaces in Module 7 (agents) and Module 9
(guardrails).

## Where LLMs fit — the part people get wrong

LLM training uses **all three**, in sequence (Lesson 2.6 has the detail):

1. **Pretraining is self-supervised** — technically supervised, but the label is free: the
   "correct answer" is simply the next token of the text itself. That is why it can use
   trillions of tokens nobody had to label, and why the model absorbs whatever the internet
   contains.
2. **Instruction tuning is supervised** — humans wrote ideal answers to prompts; the model
   imitates them.
3. **Preference tuning is reinforcement** — humans (or a judge model) rank answers, the
   ranking becomes a reward, and the model's policy shifts toward preferred answers. This is
   where helpfulness, hedging and the urge to please come from.

```check
Why can an LLM be pretrained on trillions of tokens when a supervised classifier struggles to get ten thousand labelled examples?
- LLM labs have more annotators
- * The pretraining "label" is the next token of the text itself — it costs nothing to obtain
- LLMs do not need labels at all
why: Self-supervision turns raw text into an endless supply of (input, correct-next-token) pairs. The instruction and preference stages that come later are far smaller and do need human effort.
```

## What this means for your designs

- **Have labels? Supervised, probably classic ML.** Cheap, fast, explainable enough, and
  Module 3 shows it is often the right tool over an LLM.
- **Have only raw data? Unsupervised** — clustering to discover categories, embeddings for
  search and dedup — or an LLM prompted zero-shot, then *collect labels* from its outputs and
  human corrections so you can move to supervised later.
- **Have a reward you can compute?** Rare in ordinary products, and the reward is easy to
  get wrong. Prefer supervised or rules unless the loop really is the product.
- **Using an LLM?** You are consuming the *result* of all three kinds; you do not run any of
  them at request time. Your levers are the prompt, the context and (Module 10) fine-tuning,
  which is stage 2 on your own examples.

## What a senior engineer should take from this

- **The training signal is the whole story**: answers (supervised), none (unsupervised),
  reward (reinforcement).
- **Labels are usually the constraint and the cost.** Design the labelling before the model.
- **LLMs are self-supervised, then supervised, then reinforcement-tuned** — which explains
  both their breadth and their manners.
- **Ask what signal a proposed system learns from.** If nobody can say, it is not yet a
  design.

## Exercises

1. Classify: a recommendation feed ranked by watch time; a fraud model trained on
   chargebacks; a document-similarity search; a chess engine trained by self-play; a chat
   model's "helpfulness". Name the signal for each.
2. Your product has no labels but a lot of free-text feedback. Design a two-phase plan that
   starts unsupervised or zero-shot and ends supervised.
3. A reward for a support agent is "ticket closed quickly". What behaviour will the policy
   learn, and how would you fix the reward?
