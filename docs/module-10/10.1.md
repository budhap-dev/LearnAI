## The one idea

**Fine-tuning changes how a model behaves, not what it knows.** That single sentence settles
most of the decision. If the problem is "the model doesn't know our returns policy," that is a
*knowledge* problem — the answer is retrieval (6.1), not fine-tuning, because a fine-tune bakes
facts in as of a training date and they go stale the moment the policy changes. If the problem
is "the model won't reliably produce our ticket format / house tone / terse style, no matter
how I prompt it," that is a *behaviour* problem, and fine-tuning is on the table.

So the decision is a short path, and fine-tuning is at the *end* of it, not the start:

## Walk the path

**New facts or knowledge → retrieve.** Anything that changes (prices, policies, docs, per-user
data) belongs in a retrieval layer where you can update it without retraining and cite it
(6.6). Fine-tuning for facts is the classic expensive mistake: you pay to memorise something
that is wrong by next quarter, and the model still can't tell you *where* it got the answer.

**Behaviour → try the prompt first.** A role, a few examples, a clear output contract (Module
4) get you most format, tone and style requirements for zero training cost and instant
iteration. Reach for the prompt before the GPU every time; it is cheaper, reversible, and you
can change it in a deploy.

**Behaviour the prompt can't reliably get, *and* you have data → fine-tune.** When you need a
consistent behaviour that a prompt only gets 80% of the time — a strict format under load, a
narrow classification, a domain style — and you have (or can collect) enough labelled
`(input, ideal output)` pairs, fine-tuning earns its place. It also buys efficiency: a
fine-tuned small model can match a prompted large one on a narrow task at a fraction of the
cost and latency (9.4), which is often the real reason to do it.

```check
Your support model keeps citing an outdated refund window (14 days; the real policy is now 30). What is the right fix?
- Fine-tune the model on examples that say "30 days"
- * Put the current policy in a retrieval layer and ground the answer on it — this is a knowledge problem, and a fine-tune would bake in a fact that changes again next quarter
- Add "the refund window is 30 days" to the system prompt permanently
why: Facts that change belong in retrieval, where you update them without retraining and cite the source. Fine-tuning memorises them as of a training date; the prompt line works until the next change and doesn't scale to the whole policy.
```

## The honest preconditions

Fine-tuning pays off only when several things are true at once. If any is missing, stay
prompted (and retrieved):

- **You have labelled data** — `(prompt, ideal completion)` pairs, consistent and clean.
  Quality and consistency beat quantity; a few hundred consistent examples beat thousands of
  noisy ones (10.2).
- **The behaviour is stable** — you are teaching a durable format or style, not this week's
  requirement. A fine-tune is a build artefact you version and re-run; churn makes it a
  treadmill.
- **The volume justifies it** — the training and hosting cost is amortised over enough traffic
  that the per-request saving (9.4) pays it back. For spiky or low volume, a prompted hosted
  model is cheaper all-in.
- **You can measure it** — a held-out eval set (3.3, 8.4) that says whether the fine-tune
  actually beat the prompted baseline. Without it you cannot tell improvement from regression,
  and "it feels better" is not evidence.

## RAG and fine-tuning are not rivals

The framing "RAG *or* fine-tune" is a false choice. They fix different things and compose:
retrieval supplies the *facts* at request time; fine-tuning fixes the *behaviour* — the format
the answer comes back in, the tone, the reliability of following the instruction. A mature
system often does both: a fine-tuned small model that reliably emits your grounded-answer
format, fed by a retrieval layer that keeps the facts current. Decide per *problem*, not per
*system*: is this a knowledge gap or a behaviour gap?

```check
A team wants their assistant to always answer in a strict, parseable incident-report format, and to draw on an incident knowledge base that changes weekly. What is the best split?
- Fine-tune on everything, including the incident data
- Put both the format and the incidents in retrieval
- * Fine-tune (or few-shot) for the FORMAT — a stable behaviour — and RETRIEVE the incidents — changing facts — so each mechanism does what it is good at
why: The format is a durable behaviour (fine-tune or a good prompt); the incidents are changing facts (retrieve, so they stay current and citable). They compose; forcing one mechanism to do both jobs does each worse.
```

## What a senior engineer should take from this

- **Fine-tuning changes behaviour, not knowledge.** Facts → retrieve; behaviour → prompt, then
  fine-tune only if the prompt can't get there reliably.
- **It is the last resort on a short path**, because it is the most expensive and least
  reversible option, and the prompt usually wins.
- **The preconditions are real**: labelled data, a stable target, enough volume, and a
  held-out eval — miss one and stay prompted.
- **RAG and fine-tuning compose**; decide per problem (knowledge vs behaviour), not per system.

## Exercises

1. For three real requirements on a system you know — pick one knowledge gap and two behaviour
   gaps — write down whether you'd retrieve, prompt, or fine-tune, and the one fact that
   decides each.
2. You have a prompted classifier at 88% on a golden set and a business need for 95%. List what
   you'd check *before* deciding to fine-tune, and what evidence would tell you the fine-tune
   won.
3. Estimate the break-even for a fine-tune: at what daily volume does a fine-tuned small model
   (cheaper per call, plus training + hosting cost) beat a prompted large one? Name the numbers
   you'd need.
4. Your teammate proposes fine-tuning on last month's support transcripts "so the model knows
   our product." Diagnose the mistake and propose the correct design.

## Model answers (sketch)

1. Knowledge gap → retrieve (updatable, citable). Behaviour gaps → prompt first; fine-tune only
   the one the prompt can't hold reliably. The deciding fact each time: does the requirement
   change over time (knowledge) or is it a durable how-it-answers (behaviour)?
2. Check: is 88% a prompt ceiling (try better prompting/few-shot first)? Is the golden set
   representative? Do you have clean labelled pairs? Evidence the fine-tune won: it beats the
   best prompted baseline on a *held-out* split, not the training data.
3. Need: per-call cost of each option, training cost, hosting cost/day, and daily volume.
   Break-even ≈ (training + hosting) ÷ (per-call saving). Below it, stay prompted.
4. "Know our product" is knowledge → it belongs in retrieval, kept current and cited.
   Fine-tuning transcripts would bake in stale facts and teach the model to *sound* like
   support without reliably being correct. Retrieve the product docs; fine-tune only if you
   also need a specific, stable answer *format* a prompt can't hold.
