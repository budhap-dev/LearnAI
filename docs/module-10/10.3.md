## The one idea

**Distillation trains a small "student" model to imitate a large "teacher" on your task.** You
run the expensive teacher once to label a pile of examples, then train the cheap student on
those labels. The student learns to reproduce the teacher's judgement — not ground truth, the
*teacher's output* — and on that one task it gets close, at a fraction of the cost and latency.

## The two steps

**Step 1: the teacher labels the data.** Run the big model once over a pile of your inputs and
keep its outputs. This is the one-time cost:

```code
teacher
```

```output
label
```

**Step 2: train the tiny student to imitate those labels.** The student is small and cheap —
here, a handful of parameters — and it trains to match what the teacher said:

```code
student
```

```output
distil
```

Before training, the student agrees with the teacher on only a fraction of held-out inputs.
After distillation, it agrees almost all the time — having learned the teacher's decision
boundary from nothing but the teacher's labels.

## Why this pays

```output
economics
```

The economics are the whole reason to do it. The **teacher runs once** — a fixed cost to label
a few thousand examples. The **student runs on every production request** — and it is orders of
magnitude cheaper and faster. At high volume, serving a distilled student instead of calling
the teacher per request is often the difference between a viable feature and one that costs too
much or is too slow (9.4). This is the concrete version of "a fine-tuned small model can beat a
general large one on a hot path": the student isn't smarter, it is *specialised and cheap*.

```check
A classifier runs on every inbound request at 2M requests/day. A large model is accurate but too slow and too expensive to call per request. Why does distillation help where prompting the large model does not?
- The student is more accurate than the teacher
- * You pay the large model once to label a training pile, then serve a tiny student that reproduces its judgement on every request — moving the cost from per-request to one-time, at far lower latency
- Distillation makes the large model cheaper to call
why: Distillation shifts the expensive model out of the request path. The teacher's cost is paid once during labelling; the student — cheap and fast — handles production volume. The student matches the teacher on the task, it isn't better than it.
```

## The limits

```output
when
```

A distilled student is powerful *because* it is narrow, and that narrowness is also its limit:

- **It is only as good as the teacher's labels.** Distillation copies the teacher, mistakes and
  all. A biased or wrong teacher produces a faithfully biased or wrong student.
- **Only on the distribution you labelled.** The student learns the teacher's behaviour on the
  data you showed it. Off that distribution it has no idea; a distilled ticket-classifier is not
  a chatbot.
- **Measure agreement on a held-out set** (3.3) — "does the student match the teacher on data it
  didn't train on?" — and **re-distil** when the task or the teacher changes.
- **It composes with LoRA** (10.2): distil onto a small base, then LoRA-adapt it per sub-task,
  so you get a cheap specialist you can further specialise.

```check
You distil a support-triage model from a strong teacher and it hits 97% agreement on held-out tickets. Six months later its real-world accuracy has quietly dropped. What is the most likely cause?
- The student model degraded on disk
- * Distribution drift — the incoming tickets have changed from the distribution you labelled, and the student only learned the teacher's behaviour on the old one. Re-label a fresh sample with the teacher and re-distil
- The teacher was wrong all along
why: The student is only valid on the distribution it was distilled on. As real traffic drifts from that distribution, agreement with a (correct) teacher on today's data falls. The fix is to re-label current data with the teacher and re-distil.
```

## What a senior engineer should take from this

- **Distillation = teacher labels once, student imitates and serves.** The student matches the
  teacher on the task; it is not smarter, it is cheaper and faster.
- **The economics move cost from per-request to one-time**, which is what makes a high-volume,
  latency-sensitive feature viable.
- **The student inherits the teacher's mistakes and its distribution** — measure agreement on a
  held-out set and re-distil on drift.
- **It composes with LoRA and small open models** (10.4) to build cheap, specialised serving.

## Model answers (sketch)

1. The student faithfully learns the noise too — its agreement ceiling drops toward the teacher's
   consistency. Garbage labels, garbage student: label quality is the cap on student quality.
2. Need a bigger student when a linear boundary can't fit the teacher's function — held-out
   agreement plateaus well below 100% no matter the training. Grow the student until held-out
   agreement stops improving.
3. Monitor held-out agreement (against fresh teacher labels) and downstream business metrics;
   fire a re-distill when agreement on a recent sample drops below threshold; fresh labels come
   from running the teacher on a new sample of current traffic.
4. Distillation makes a *small, cheap* model that imitates a big one on a task; LoRA *adapts* a
   base's behaviour with a tiny adapter. Together: distil to a small base for cost, then LoRA-
   adapt that base per sub-task.
