## The one idea

A model call sends your data somewhere and often gets logged on the way. That makes every LLM
feature a **data-processing system**, subject to the same obligations as any other — plus a
few the model creates. None of this is the model's concern; it is the system's, which means
it is yours.

Four questions a review must answer:

1. **Where does the prompt go?** Which vendor, which region, under what data-processing terms?
2. **What is kept, and for how long?** Prompts, responses, traces, stored "memory".
3. **Whose data trains what?** Is your content used to train the vendor's model? Is a user's
   data used to fine-tune yours, and did they consent?
4. **Who is accountable for a decision?** When the feature affects a person, a human — not the
   model — must own the outcome.

## Minimise, redact, scope

The cheapest privacy control is to not send or store the data in the first place.

- **Minimise PII in prompts.** Send the model what it needs to do the task, not the whole
  customer record. A summariser needs the ticket text, not the account's card number. Every
  field you omit is a field that cannot leak (9.5's LLM06).
- **Redact in logs.** Logs outlive requests and have wider access. The tracing you built in
  5.7 must strip obvious PII (emails, card numbers, names) before it lands — the redaction
  step there is a privacy control, not a nicety. Raw prompts, if kept at all, go in a
  short-retention store with tight access, never the general application log.
- **Scope stored memory.** The fact store from 7.5 is personal data: hold only what you would
  show a user on a "what we know about you" page, give it provenance and timestamps, and make
  it deletable.

```check
Your support assistant logs the full prompt (including the customer's message and account context) to your standard application logs "for debugging". What is wrong, and what is the fix?
- Nothing — debugging needs the full prompt
- * Logs outlive requests and have broad access, so raw PII in them is a disclosure risk (LLM06); redact PII before logging and keep any raw prompts in a short-retention, access-controlled store
why: Debuggability does not justify PII sprawl. Redact by default (5.7); keep the rare raw-prompt need behind retention limits and access control.
```

## Retention, region, and training

- **Retention.** Decide, per data type, how long prompts, responses and traces are kept, and
  enforce it. "We keep everything forever" is a breach waiting to be discovered; retention
  limits shrink the blast radius of any future incident.
- **Region.** Some data must be processed in a specific jurisdiction. This is a routing and
  vendor-selection decision (9.2, 1.5): use a model/endpoint in the required region, and make
  the gateway enforce it. "The model is great but processes in the wrong region" is a
  disqualifier, not a footnote.
- **Training on your data.** Read the vendor's terms: on some tiers your prompts may be used
  to improve their models. For sensitive data, use the endpoint/terms that exclude it. And if
  *you* fine-tune on user data (Module 10), that is processing that needs a lawful basis and,
  usually, consent.

```check
A new model is clearly the best on your eval, but its only endpoint processes data in a region your compliance team prohibits for customer data. What is the right call?
- Use it — it's the best model, and evals are what matter
- * It is disqualified for that data: regional processing is a hard constraint, not a quality trade-off; route non-sensitive traffic to it if useful, and keep customer data on a compliant endpoint
why: Compliance constraints are binary gates, not points on the quality curve. The gateway (9.2) can route by data sensitivity so a model can be used where it is permitted and not where it is not.
```

## Governance: accountability and documentation

Technology aside, an AI feature needs the same governance as any consequential system:

- **Human accountability for decisions.** When the feature affects a person — a refund, a
  claim, a moderation call — a human owns the decision of record, with the model as an aid
  (Lesson 3.1's rule, now a compliance requirement). "The AI decided" is not an accountable
  answer, and under regimes like the **EU AI Act**, higher-risk uses carry explicit
  obligations for human oversight, transparency and record-keeping.
- **Document the feature.** A short model card for *your* feature: what it does, what data it
  uses, its known limitations and evaluation results (Module 8), and the controls protecting
  it (9.5). This is what an auditor, a new team member, or an incident review reads.
- **Know your risk tier.** Most internal tooling is low-risk; features that make decisions
  about people's access, money, employment or rights are not. The higher the tier, the more
  oversight, testing and documentation the law and good practice require.

## What a senior engineer should take from this

- **Every LLM feature is a data-processing system** — minimise PII in prompts, redact it in
  logs, scope and time-limit stored memory.
- **Retention, region and training terms are hard constraints**, enforced at the gateway and
  in vendor selection — not quality trade-offs.
- **A human owns any decision about a person;** the model is an aid. Document the feature like
  a data system (a model card) and know its risk tier.
- **Privacy controls are mostly the security controls from 9.5** seen from the data side —
  the same fencing, scoping and redaction.

## Exercises

1. For a feature you know, write down the four answers: where the prompt goes, what is
   retained and for how long, whether data trains a model, and who owns a decision.
2. Audit its logs for PII. What is being logged that should be redacted, and where should the
   rare raw-prompt need live instead?
3. Draft the one-page model card for the feature: purpose, data used, limitations, eval
   results, controls. What was hardest to fill in — and is that a gap?
4. Classify three of your team's AI features by risk tier (internal tooling vs decisions about
   people). What extra oversight does the highest-tier one need?

## Model answers (sketch)

1. Often the weakest answer is retention ("we keep everything") or accountability ("the
   feature decides") — both are findings to fix.
2. Full prompts and responses in app logs are the usual offender; redact PII inline (5.7) and
   move any raw capture to a short-retention, access-controlled store.
3. "Limitations" and "eval results" are hardest when there is no golden set — which is itself
   the finding (Module 8).
4. Internal search = low; a tool that auto-approves or denies customer requests = high, needing
   human oversight, an audit trail, and documented evaluation.
