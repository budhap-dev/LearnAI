## The one idea

A model bill has two failure modes: a slow drift upward nobody notices, and a sudden spike
from one loop, one tenant or one prompt. Both are prevented by the same three habits, and
none of them involves the model:

```diagram
model-router
```

1. **A cap per request** — input tokens (truncate deliberately, Lesson 2.5) and `max_tokens`
   for output. One request can cost at most *this much*.
2. **A ledger per tenant per day** — when a customer (or an internal job) crosses it, degrade
   first (smaller model, shorter answers, queue), refuse last, and alert before either.
3. **A router** — classification, extraction and routing itself go to a small model;
   judgement-heavy work goes to a large one. The rule is code; you tune it with evals.

## Routing by the shape of the task

```code
router
```

```output
routing
```

The router is four lines and it is deliberately boring: *what kind of task is this, and how
big is the input?* Small models are genuinely good at classification and extraction
(Lesson 2.8); the large model's edge is judgement, long reasoning and open-ended
generation. Send each what it is good at, and **measure each route on the golden set**
(Lesson 8.4) before moving a task down a tier — "it seemed fine" is not a measurement.

Common refinements, once the basic rule works: route by *confidence* (small model first, escalate
when its structured output fails validation or says `not_found`); route by *tenant tier*;
route by *latency budget* (a user waiting gets the fast model).

Read the day: Carol's 9,500-token request was rejected at the door (the per-request cap —
truncate or ask her to narrow it; Lesson 2.5), Alice burned through most of her daily
allowance on a mix of small and large calls, and nobody was surprised. **Degrade before you
refuse**: a user over budget still gets an answer from the small model, or a shorter one,
or a queued one. Refusal is for abuse, not for a good customer on a busy day.

```check
A tenant hits their daily budget at 2pm. The best default policy is…
- Refuse all further requests until midnight
- * Degrade — small model / shorter answers / queue — and alert the account owner; refuse only for abuse
- Silently keep serving and bill them later
why: A hard refusal turns a cost control into an outage for a paying customer. Degradation keeps the product working while bounding spend; alerts make the trade-off visible.
```

## What a senior engineer should take from this

- **Cap per request, ledger per tenant, route by task shape** — three rules in code.
- **Degrade before refuse; alert before degrade.**
- **Route with evidence**: measure each route on the golden set; revisit when models change.
- **A ceiling on every request and every tenant** is what turns "the bill" into a number you
  can forecast.

## Exercises

1. Extend the router with a confidence escalation: try the small model with a schema; if
   validation fails or `not_found`, retry on the large model. What does it do to cost and to
   the validation-pass rate?
2. Your ledger is per user per day. A batch job runs under a service account and burns the
   whole budget at 00:05. What changes?
3. Write the alert rules: per-request cost p95, per-tenant spend vs cap, and share of traffic
   on the large model. What threshold and what action for each?
4. Argue the other side: when is "everything to the large model" the right call?

## Model answers (sketch)

1. Most requests stay cheap; the few that escalate cost two calls; overall pass rate rises
   because the large model catches the hard cases. Measure the escalation rate.
2. Separate budgets per principal and per *kind* of principal (users vs jobs); jobs go to
   batch (Lesson 5.5) with their own ledger.
3. p95 cost per request above X → page; tenant at 80% of cap → notify owner, at 100% →
   degrade; large-model share above Y → review the router.
4. Low volume, high stakes, judgement-heavy, no eval set yet: pay for quality first, optimise
   once you can measure.
